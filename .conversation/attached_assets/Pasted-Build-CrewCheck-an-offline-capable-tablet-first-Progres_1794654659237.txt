1. PRODUCT PURPOSE

CrewCheck helps astronauts:
- Assess their own health.
- Assist a crewmate who may be ill or injured.
- Follow understandable measurement and emergency instructions.
- Coordinate available helpers.
- Understand possible relationships between crew observations and habitat conditions.
- Maintain routines and follow-up tasks.
- Prepare an accurate medical handoff when support from Earth is delayed or unavailable.

The central concept is:
PERSON + CREW + HABITAT.

The signature demonstration is a crewmember developing sharp chest pain while the application also surfaces potentially relevant habitat observations.

Emergency assistance and habitat investigation run in parallel when helpers are available. Environmental analysis must never delay patient assistance or dismiss symptoms.

This is a hackathon research and training prototype. It is not validated medical equipment or flight-qualified software.

2. PRODUCT AND TECHNICAL DECISIONS

Build a PWA, not an Electron application or Windows-only executable.

Preferred implementation:
- React and TypeScript.
- Vite or an equivalent lightweight setup compatible with Replit.
- A consistent component system and responsive styling.
- IndexedDB for local records, using a maintained wrapper if helpful.
- A service worker for offline application assets and procedure content.
- A small server-side endpoint only for optional AI assistance.
- A lightweight chart library if needed.

Replit is the development and hosting environment. The core app must operate on the user's device after initial online preparation.

Do not make emergency access, records, assessments, or handoff generation depend on:
- Replit's database.
- A remote login.
- A cloud AI model.
- A live NASA API.
- External fonts, images, or scripts that have not been bundled or cached.

Do not build authentication, billing, live wearable integrations, simultaneous multi-device synchronization, or native mobile packaging for this prototype.

Use one device containing fictional crew profiles. Keep components reusable for future platforms.

Support configurable profiles for:

- Communication availability.
- Configured communication delay.
- Routine schedules.
- Applicable procedure and measurement-guide variants.

Do not infer communication delay solely from the word “spacecraft.”
Do not treat spacecraft as synonymous with Mars.
Do not apply Earth positioning instructions unchanged to microgravity.
Do not create unsupported lunar or microgravity medical procedures.

Fully implement and demonstrate one fictional space-station profile.
Provide spacecraft and lunar-base configurations that demonstrate equipment, location, schedule, and communication differences.

Where a procedure is not supported for a selected environment, explicitly explain that limitation and provide the supported escalation route. Never silently substitute an unsuitable procedure.

All sample inventories, layouts, and schedules must be labeled fictional.

4. DESIGN AND EASE OF USE

Design for a landscape tablet, with excellent laptop support and a usable narrow-screen layout.

Use:
- Large touch targets, approximately 48 CSS pixels or larger for primary controls.
- Comfortable text sizes and clear spacing.
- High contrast and visible keyboard focus.
- Text labels alongside icons.
- Status labels alongside colors.
- Plain language appropriate for non-doctors.
- Short instructions, one main action per emergency step.
- Progressive disclosure for explanations and scientific detail.
- Consistent patient identity and mission context.

Avoid:
- Tiny controls.
- Dense medical dashboards.
- Long forms before urgent help.
- Decorative animations during emergency tasks.
- Unexplained abbreviations.
- Fake precision or arbitrary health scores.
- “Healthy” labels based solely on missing or limited data.

Use operational statuses such as:
- Not assessed.
- Check-in due.
- Observation recorded.
- Active incident.
- Data unavailable.
- Reading needs confirmation.

Visual style:
Professional, calm, and suitable for a space-health application.
Use a restrained space-inspired palette, clear cards, and a simple habitat schematic.
Do not imitate a cluttered spacecraft cockpit.
Do not use NASA branding in a way that implies endorsement.

Keep a persistent Emergency control accessible throughout the app.
Emergency entry must not require completing a form or selecting every patient detail first.

Include an unobtrusive but persistent “Simulation / Research prototype” indicator.

5. INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE

Implement eight main areas. Related functions may use tabs or drawers to prevent navigation clutter.

Show when a reading is old, missing, simulated, or user-reported.
Do not interpret every deviation from baseline as dangerous.
Do not display an overall medical fitness score.

B. GUIDED ASSESSMENT

Support two modes:
- Self-assessment.
- Assessing a crewmate.

Record:
- Patient.
- Observer/helper.
- Symptoms in the user's own words.
- Symptom onset.
- Location.
- Recent activity.
- Relevant contextual observations.
- Available measurements.

Distinguish:
- Patient-reported symptoms.
- Helper-observed signs.
- Device measurements.
- AI-suggested information awaiting confirmation.

Offer:
- Structured symptom buttons.
- Free-text notes.
- Manual fields.
- Optional AI extraction.

Allow “Not sure,” “Unable to assess,” and “Not available.”
Never replace uncertainty with a normal value.

Selecting acute chest pain must expose the urgent pathway immediately.
Do not require vital signs before entering emergency assistance.

C. MEASUREMENT GUIDES

Create two complete interface workflows:
- Blood-pressure measurement.
- Pulse-oximeter measurement.

Each guide contains:
- Why the measurement matters.
- Required equipment.
- Device and environment applicability.
- Illustrations or simple diagrams.
- Preparation and positioning instructions where supported.
- Measurement entry with units.
- Reading-quality questions.
- Timestamp and operator identity.
- An explanation of limitations.
- The supported next action.

Blood pressure records systolic and diastolic values separately in mmHg.
Pulse-oximeter readings record SpO₂ in percent and pulse in beats per minute if available.

Do not imply that an ordinary tablet camera provides validated blood pressure or oxygen readings.

Do not ask users to delay urgent care to complete routine measurement preparation.
Do not dismiss serious symptoms because a device reading appears normal.

Include “Equipment unavailable” and “Unable to obtain a reading.”
Validate data types and units. Any technical plausibility checks must be distinguished from clinical interpretation.

Use source-backed draft content, with review status visible in the evidence drawer.
If a device-specific or mission-specific instruction is unavailable, explicitly identify the gap.

D. ASSESSMENT REVIEW

Show:
- Confirmed observations.
- Missing or uncertain information.
- Descriptive trends.
- Relevant source-linked explanations.
- Next steps from the configured scenario logic.
- Follow-up task creation.
- Handoff preparation.

Do not invent diagnoses, personalized risk percentages, or clinical thresholds.

E. EMERGENCY ASSISTANT

Implement a deterministic chest-pain demonstration with:
- Immediate onboard-help and medical-contact prompts.
- Responsiveness and normal-breathing observations.
- Appropriate escalation when the patient becomes unresponsive or breathing changes.
- A persistent “Condition changed” control.
- Equipment availability and location.
- One instruction per screen.
- Action acknowledgement.
- Event timestamps and elapsed time.
- Persistent incident state.
- Clear missing-information handling.

Implement explicit states and transitions, not an AI-generated conversation controlling the emergency.

Use generic, source-backed initial first-aid concepts and clearly marked simulation content. Do not fabricate NASA-approved protocols.

For resuscitation escalation, direct the user to the applicable trained crew protocol and available device prompts. Do not invent microgravity CPR techniques, medication doses, invasive procedures, or unsupported treatment instructions.

Treatment-specific content without an appropriate source and review must remain explicitly unavailable rather than being generated by AI.

Each procedure step should include:
- Step ID.
- Trigger or precondition.
- Exact instruction.
- Expected observation or completion.
- Equipment requirements.
- Applicability.
- Supported alternatives.
- Next states.
- Source references.
- Review status and version.

Allow these responses where relevant:
- Completed.
- Equipment unavailable.
- Unable to assess.
- Not sure.
- Condition changed.

Do not permit skipping a safety-critical step as though it were completed.
Record corrections without silently deleting prior events.

CREW COORDINATION
- Identify available helpers and training metadata.
- Assign tasks within the scripted, reviewed scenario.
- Identify patient-facing and equipment/communication tasks.
- Record who completed each task.
- Preserve a single-helper path where supported.
- Allow another helper to take over.
- Show completed actions, current step, and unresolved observations.

This is a shared-device workflow. Do not add multi-device networking.

F. HABITAT MAP AND “WHAT CHANGED?” TIMELINE

Create a simple schematic with three zones for the main demo:
- Exercise area.
- Living/sleep area.
- Medical/work area.

Show:
- Last-reported crew locations and their timestamps.
- Symptoms associated with locations and times.
- Available environmental readings.
- Sensor identity, units, timestamps, and data quality.
- Missing or stale readings.
- Possible overlaps in observations.

Location is manually reported or simulated. Do not imply real-time tracking.

The timeline lets users inspect:
- Earlier observations.
- Recent changes.
- Current incident context.

Keep routine time-series trends separate from emergency severity.

Implement one scripted shared-environment scenario:
- An earlier fictional symptom report in a zone.
- A simulated environmental reading change.
- A later patient incident with overlapping location/time.
- An inspectable notice that the observations overlap.

Language should say:
“Possible shared environmental concern.”
“Observations overlap in time and location.”
“Cause undetermined.”

Never say:
“The cabin caused this medical event.”
“CO₂ poisoning confirmed.”
“Radiation sickness detected.”
unless supported by an actual validated diagnostic process, which this prototype does not implement.

Each connection must reveal the evidence behind it.
Missing data must reduce certainty, not create reassurance.

Any demo sensor thresholds must be explicitly labeled simulation triggers, not operational safety limits.

The app must not control ventilation, oxygen, pressure, shielding, or other life-support hardware.

6. SCIENCE AND CONTENT RULES

Use this explanation structure:
Why it matters → equipment → how to measure → check quality → interpretation limits → supported next action.

Preserve these distinctions:
- Hazard versus symptom.
- Symptom versus diagnosis.
- Measured versus reported.
- Correlation versus cause.
- Environmental measurement versus personal exposure.
- Research data versus live patient data.
- Demonstration threshold versus clinical threshold.

Do not:
- Estimate bone density from days in space.
- Infer radiation dose from symptoms.
- Infer immune function from temperature alone.
- Invent medication dosing.
- Claim medical validation or NASA endorsement.
- Treat a simulated habitat association as clinical evidence.

Mission context:
- Communication delay varies by destination and circumstances.
- A Mars day is approximately 24 hours and 39.5 minutes; this is not extra daylight.
- Lunar surface gravity is approximately one-sixth of Earth's.
- Microgravity procedures require appropriate supporting instructions.
- Do not use the handwritten radiation range from our notes as an alarm threshold.

Fine motor skills, vision, fluid shifts, nutrition, pharmaceuticals, laboratory analysis, and resource recycling are future research/content areas. Do not build additional diagnostic modules for them in this first release.
